A critique is not a review, an approval, or a meeting where the loudest
opinion wins. It is forty-five minutes spent making one piece of work better,
and it falls apart without structure.

This is ours, unchanged for about eighteen months now.

## Before the room

The person presenting writes three lines and posts them the day before:

1. What problem this solves, for whom.
2. What they have already decided and are not reopening today.
3. The one question they want help with.

The second line is the one people skip and the one that saves the session.
Without it, half the time goes to relitigating a decision made three weeks ago
by someone who is not in the room.

## In the room

- **Five minutes: context.** Read the three lines aloud. No slides.
- **Ten minutes: silence.** Everyone looks at the work and writes notes
  privately. This is not politeness; it stops the first speaker from
  anchoring the other five.
- **Twenty minutes: the question.** Only the question. Anything else goes in a
  parked list.
- **Ten minutes: the parked list,** if there is time. Usually there is not,
  and that is fine.

The silent ten minutes was the hardest thing to introduce and the single
change that improved the sessions most.

## How feedback is phrased

We ask for observations before prescriptions:

> "My eye went to the button before the heading" is worth more than "make the
> heading bigger."

The first tells the designer something true about their work. The second
hands them a solution to a problem they now have to reverse-engineer.

## Who decides

The presenter. Not the room, not the most senior person present, not
consensus. A critique produces information; the person doing the work
produces the decision. Writing this down ended a whole category of quiet
resentment.

## What we watch for

Two failure modes recur:

- **The rescue.** Someone starts redesigning live. Kind, well meant, and it
  ends the critique.
- **The pile-on.** Three people agree with the first criticism before anyone
  checks whether it was right.

Naming both in advance means anyone can call them without it being personal.
Usually a single word does it.
